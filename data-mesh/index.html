Data mesh: domain ownership, federated governance

A data mesh treats data products the way a microservices platform treats services: autonomous, owned, contract-bound. The bus matrix becomes the data product catalog — each row is a product, each conformed dimension is a shared product, and the platform team provides the infrastructure that lets domain teams publish and consume without central bottlenecks.

The bus matrix IS the data product catalog

In a monolithic warehouse, the bus matrix is a planning artefact that lives in a slide deck. In a data mesh, it is the runtime catalog. Each row is a discoverable data product with an owner, a schema, a SLA and a consumption API. Each conformed dimension is a shared product owned by the master-data service. The matrix is no longer a picture — it is an index.

The deeper shift is in who owns what. In a centralised warehouse, a central data team owns everything; domain teams hand over data and lose interest. In a mesh, domain teams own their data products the way they own their services. Quality goes up because the team closest to the source is accountable for it. The central team becomes a platform team — smaller, sharper, and focused on infrastructure rather than data content.

The four pillars

Zhamak Dehghani’s original four pillars, mapped to concrete platform capabilities.

Domain-oriented ownership

The team that produces a data product owns it end-to-end — schema, quality, SLAs, evolution. There is no central data team that “ingests” the domain’s data; the domain publishes it as a product, with the same care a product team ships a user-facing feature.

Data as a product

Every data product has a named owner, a versioned schema, a SLA, a quality contract, a license and a discoverability entry in the catalog. Consumers treat data products the way they treat external APIs — with contracts, not assumptions.

Self-serve platform

A platform team provides the infrastructure to publish, discover, secure and observe data products. Domain teams focus on their data; the platform handles the mechanics. This is the “data infrastructure as a product” pillar.

Federated governance

Global rules (naming, classification, retention, access patterns) are set centrally; enforcement is local. The governance service publishes policies; each domain service enforces them. Standards emerge through the conformed dimensions, not through top-down mandate.

Federated governance in practice

Federated governance is the hardest pillar to get right. Too much central control and you have re-created the monolith with extra steps. Too little and the mesh fragments into incompatible data products that cannot be joined.

This platform threads the needle by governing the conformed dimensions centrally (because they are the join keys) and leaving the fact-shaped data products to the domains. The governance service publishes a small set of policies: naming conventions, classification tags, retention rules, access patterns, and the conformed dimension definitions. Each domain enforces these locally; the platform reports compliance back to the governance service for monitoring.

New conformed dimensions are proposed by domains and ratified by a federation council that meets monthly. This is slow on purpose — conformance is a one-way door, and getting it wrong locks in inconsistency for years.
